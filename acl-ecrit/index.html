Le commentary

The commentary

Le commentary est un cousin de l'analyse linéaire française, mais avec deux différences majeures : il s'organise par idées (et non ligne à ligne), et il exige de replacer sans cesse l'extrait dans l'économie de la pièce entière. C'est un exercice de close reading théâtral : tu analyses un texte écrit pour être joué.

The commentary is a cousin of the French analyse linéaire, but with two major differences: it is organised by ideas (not line by line), and it constantly requires you to place the passage within the design of the whole play. It is an exercise in theatrical close reading: you are analysing a text written to be performed.

Part A et Part B : ne pas se saborder Part A and Part B: don’t undercut yourself

Les deux volets portent sur la même pièce mais sont notés séparément. Garde pour Part B tes grandes lectures d’ensemble et tes exemples pris ailleurs dans la pièce : Part A se juge sur ta capacité à rester dans l’extrait, mot à mot. Un renvoi bref au reste de la pièce est bienvenu ; un développement thématique, non — il fera doublon et te coûtera deux fois.

Both halves deal with the same play but are marked separately. Save your broad readings and your examples from elsewhere in the play for Part B: Part A is judged on your ability to stay inside the passage, word by word. A brief glance outwards is welcome; a thematic digression is not — it will duplicate Part B and cost you twice.

Les cinq étapes

The five steps

  1. Situer l'extrait. Où en est la pièce ? Que vient-il de se passer, que va-t-il se passer ? Pourquoi ce moment est-il un pivot (révélation, retournement, ironie dramatique) ? Situate the passage. Where are we in the play? What has just happened, what is about to happen? Why is this moment a hinge (revelation, reversal, dramatic irony)?
  2. Repérer le mouvement interne. L'extrait a sa propre dynamique : un basculement de ton, une montée de tension, un changement vers/quitter le vers ou la prose. Ce mouvement te donne souvent ton plan. Identify the internal movement. The passage has its own dynamic: a shift in tone, a rise in tension, a switch into or out of verse or prose. That movement often gives you your plan.
  3. Formuler une thèse. Une phrase qui dit ce que l'extrait accomplit : « This passage stages the collapse of X's certainty… ». Toute l'analyse servira cette thèse. Frame a thesis. One sentence stating what the passage achieves: "This passage stages the collapse of X's certainty…". The entire analysis will serve that thesis.
  4. Développer 3–4 paragraphes analytiques organisés par idées, chacun en PEA (Point, Evidence, Analysis), mêlant langage et théâtralité. Develop 3–4 analytical paragraphs organised by ideas, each following PEA (Point, Evidence, Analysis), weaving language and theatricality together.
  5. Conclure vers la pièce. Élargis : que change cet extrait pour la trajectoire des personnages et les thèmes majeurs ? En quoi éclaire-t-il l'œuvre entière ? Conclude towards the play. Widen out: what does this passage change for the characters' trajectories and the major themes? How does it illuminate the whole work?

Le paragraphe PEA modèle

The model PEA paragraph

Point — une idée interprétative claire en tête de paragraphe. Evidence — une citation courte, intégrée à ta propre phrase entre guillemets. Analysis — au moins deux tiers du paragraphe : nomme le procédé, décris son effet, rattache-le à ta thèse et, si possible, à un écho ailleurs dans la pièce. Termine par une phrase qui fait le lien avec le paragraphe suivant.

Point — one clear interpretive idea at the top of the paragraph. Evidence — a short quotation, embedded in your own sentence within quotation marks. Analysis — at least two thirds of the paragraph: name the device, describe its effect, connect it to your thesis and, where possible, to an echo elsewhere in the play. End with a sentence that bridges into the next paragraph.

L'essay

The essay

L'essay à l'anglaise ne ressemble ni à la dissertation française ni au commentaire composé : c'est une démonstration pilotée par une thèse. Dès l'introduction, tu annonces ta réponse à la question ; chaque paragraphe apporte ensuite une pièce au dossier. Le correcteur A-Level valorise l'engagement critique : une position claire, nuancée, et défendue par le texte.

The English-style essay is neither the French dissertation nor the commentaire composé: it is a thesis-driven argument. From the introduction onwards, you announce your answer to the question; each paragraph then adds a piece to the case. The A-Level examiner rewards critical engagement: a clear, nuanced position defended by the text.

Construire l'essay en cinq temps

Building the essay in five moves

  1. Décortiquer la question (5 min). Souligne les mots-clés, définis les termes ambigus, repère la portée exacte (« in the play as a whole » ≠ « in this act »). Unpack the question (5 min). Underline the key words, define ambiguous terms, pin down the exact scope ("in the play as a whole" ≠ "in this act").
  2. Planifier (10–15 min). Thèse en une phrase + 3–4 arguments ordonnés du plus évident au plus fin + les citations qui iront avec chacun. Un plan solide fait gagner du temps, il n'en fait pas perdre. Plan (10–15 min). A one-sentence thesis + 3–4 arguments ordered from most obvious to most subtle + the quotations that go with each. A solid plan saves time, it does not waste it.
  3. Introduire court. Une phrase de contexte maximum, puis la thèse, puis l'annonce des axes. Pas de biographie de l'auteur, pas de généralités sur « la littérature ». Introduce briefly. One sentence of context at most, then the thesis, then the line of argument. No author biography, no generalities about "literature".
  4. Dérouler en PEEL. Point, Evidence, Explanation, Link : chaque paragraphe ouvre sur un argument, cite court et intégré, analyse en profondeur, et referme en reliant à la question. Develop in PEEL. Point, Evidence, Explanation, Link: each paragraph opens on an argument, quotes briefly and in an embedded way, analyses in depth, and closes by linking back to the question.
  5. Conclure en tranchant. Réponds réellement à la question posée (surtout aux « how far »), sans répéter mot pour mot, et ouvre si possible sur la vision d'ensemble de l'auteur. Conclude decisively. Actually answer the question asked (especially "how far" ones), without repeating yourself word for word, and if possible open onto the writer's larger vision.
Note de l'examinateur — citer comme un A-Level Examiner's note — quoting like an A-Level

Les citations doivent être courtes (quelques mots suffisent), exactes (apprises par cœur : pas de texte autorisé pour les essays) et intégrées grammaticalement à ta phrase. Mieux vaut huit micro-citations tissées dans l'argumentation que deux gros blocs recopiés.

Quotations must be short (a few words are enough), exact (learnt by heart: no open book for the essays) and grammatically embedded in your sentence. Eight micro-quotations woven into the argument beat two big copied-out blocks.

Les pièges classiques

Classic pitfalls

  • Le récit : raconter l'intrigue au lieu d'argumenter. Test : si une phrase pourrait figurer dans un résumé Wikipédia, coupe-la.
  • Narrative retelling: recounting the plot instead of arguing. Test: if a sentence could appear in a Wikipedia summary, cut it.
  • Le hors-sujet poli : réciter le cours sur le thème voisin plutôt que traiter la question exacte. Reviens à la formulation du sujet à chaque fin de paragraphe.
  • The polite off-topic: reciting your notes on a neighbouring theme rather than tackling the exact question. Return to the question's wording at the end of every paragraph.
  • L'affirmation sans preuve : toute idée doit s'appuyer sur un moment précis du texte, cité ou référencé.
  • Assertion without evidence: every idea must rest on a precise moment of the text, quoted or referenced.
  • La neutralité : un essay sans position est un essay sans note haute. Ose une lecture, puis nuance-la.
  • Fence-sitting: an essay without a stance is an essay without a top mark. Commit to a reading, then qualify it.

KP20

Ce que le barème officiel appelle « work of distinction », traduit en cinq exigences concrètes : (1) argument et preuves tissés inséparablement — jamais une idée puis une citation posée à côté ; (2) une hiérarchie — les lignes de force développées, le secondaire traité d'une phrase ; (3) l'ambiguïté maintenue sans réduire l'œuvre — trancher sans simplifier ; (4) les effets littéraires discutés en relation avec le sens, jamais catalogués ; (5) une langue qui porte une réponse personnelle vivante, pas une simple transmission d'idées.

What the official scheme calls 'work of distinction', translated into five concrete demands: (1) argument and evidence woven inseparably — never an idea with a quotation laid beside it; (2) a hierarchy — central lines developed, secondary matter dealt with in a sentence; (3) ambiguity sustained without reducing the text — deciding without simplifying; (4) literary effects discussed in relation to meaning, never catalogued; (5) language carrying a vivid personal response, not a mere transmission of ideas.

Sujet A — essay modèle intégral, annoté

Question A — full model essay, annotated

'In Dancing at Lughnasa, words deceive; only dance tells the truth.' How far do you agree?

The claim captures the play's most visible design: Friel builds a world in which speech is performance and the body is confession. Yet to agree entirely would be to reduce a play that never simplifies. I shall argue that Dancing at Lughnasa does systematically discredit the spoken word and entrust revelation to movement — but with two crucial qualifications: it is not language itself that lies so much as a society in which the truth may not be said; and in the end it is words, Michael's words, that alone preserve what the dancing meant.

A three-move introduction: the claim reframed, a firm stance ('I shall argue'), and the two qualifications that will structure what follows. Not a line of biography or generality — the scheme rewards entering the discussion at once.

Speech in Ballybeg is first of all a performance, and its virtuoso is Gerry Evans. His talk is a salesman's — four brochures distributed and nothing sold, a cow with a single horn solemnly reported, marriage proposed at every visit — and the play lets Christina deliver the verdict his charm deserves: [fiche 10]. What matters here is the diagnosis, not the reproach: 'nature' absolves him of intention and thereby condemns speech itself, since even sincere words cannot bind him. Her instruction that follows — no more words, only dancing down the lane — is therefore less a lover's whim than a philosophy: where language cannot promise, the body must answer for it. Even Gerry's one apparent commitment, enlisting for Spain, is emptied in the telling: he answers 'No' to every question of conviction and settles for what he calls a named destination. Kate's public speech works the same way from the other side of respectability: her veto of the harvest dance invokes no doctrine, only the laughter of the countryside — language as the voice of the watching parish rather than of the self.

Against this counterfeit currency the play sets the body, and it does so with theatrical means as much as with plot. The famous dance of the five sisters is scripted in a stage direction of extraordinary length — a prose-poem in which Maggie masks her face with flour, order is consciously subverted, and the movement is made, in Friel's own word, 'grotesque'. Two details of that choreography carry the argument. The sisters enter the dance in inverse order of docility, Kate last; and Kate, the play's most verbal character, dances entirely without sound: her truest utterance is the one that uses no words at all. The dance's ugliness matters as much as its energy — truth here is not beautiful, it is accurate. The same logic governs the quietest scene in the play, the parents' second dance in the garden, which the narrator remembers thus: [fiche 14]. The sibilance of that sentence is itself a kind of choreography; and the word Michael chooses — 'ceremony' — quietly marries this wordless wedding to the rituals of Ryanga and of the back hills. Dance, in this play, is the sincerity test every character passes or fails.

Quoting a stage direction and discussing two of its staging details (order of entry, Kate's silence) proves awareness of the text as theatre — an explicit top-band criterion. Note too the stylistic analysis of the quotation ('sibilance'): effect tied to meaning, never catalogued.

The deepest version of the argument, however, is Father Jack, in whom movement does not oppose language but resurrects it. Jack returns from Uganda with his English in ruins, and Friel writes his aphasia as lexical suspense: hunting for the word that would name a ritual killing with dancing and incantation, he tries and rejects 'exhibition', 'demonstration', 'spectacle', and surrenders — until, a page later, the gesture of giving recalls the word 'ceremony' in triumph. The scene is the play's thesis in miniature: the English Jack lost is precisely the vocabulary of shared sacredness, and it is a remembered movement of the hands, not a dictionary, that restores it. The close of Act One stages the same idea structurally: while Michael's voice tells the future — the factory, the flight, the deaths — Jack performs his shuffling Ryangan dance with the kite sticks. The future arrives in words; the past persists in the body; the audience watches both at once.

And yet the claim, taken whole, mistakes a social condition for a metaphysical one. Words in this play are not inherently false; they are forbidden. When Agnes finally speaks her desire plainly — [fiche 03] — nothing in the language deceives: the short declaratives, the reclaimed argument of age, the naked 'I want' are as truthful as any dance in the play. What follows is not exposure of a lie but suppression of a truth: Kate's veto, in the name of what the countryside would say. Rose makes the complementary point from below. Ending her account of Lough Anna — [fiche 18] — the play's 'simple' sister performs its most sovereign speech-act: not telling, but deciding what shall be told. Language, withheld, becomes power. The problem, then, is not the medium but the world: Ballybeg has made honest speech unaffordable, and the body dances because the mouth is policed.

Version visant KP17–20
A KP17–20 version

A play may be called feminist in at least three distinct senses: because it exposes the conditions that constrain women's lives, because it endorses their escape from those conditions, or because it grants women the authority to narrate their own experience. To call the play feminist is therefore accurate about its diagnosis and misleading about its politics.

Kate's authority inside the cottage is real, and it is borrowed. She polices her sisters' speech — "shut up, would you?" — with a sharpness that has no equivalent anywhere else in her language, and the play is careful to show where that sharpness comes from. Version visant KP17–20
A KP17–20 version

Chris's decision to wear lipstick is the point at which the play's feminism becomes hardest to settle, and the hesitancy is written into the line itself. "I think I just might" stacks three hedges — a verb of opinion, an adverb of minimisation, a modal of the weakest kind — around an action that costs nothing and harms no one. She is asking permission of herself, and there is nobody else in the room from whom permission could be sought.

Read one way, this is capitulation: she is making herself ready for a man who has already left once and will leave again, and the lipstick is a mask over the face the play has told us alters when she is happy. Read another, it is the only act of self-fashioning her circumstances permit. She has no wage, no property, no prospect of marriage and a child born outside it; the tube of lipstick is the single object in the cottage over which she exercises a decision. The play declines to tell us which reading to take, and that refusal is the argument: the same object is at once the instrument of her subjection and the last territory she governs.

A KP17–20 version

Gerry's charm is exposed not by anything a character says about him but by repetition across the two acts. "You're a great dancer, Aggie" is, word for word, the compliment already paid to Chris; the audience holds both scenes together and hears the recycling, while each sister hears the compliment only once. This is dramatic irony working structurally rather than locally, and it achieves something no speech could: it implicates us, since we know Agnes is being handled and we watch her go on dancing anyway.

What the moment establishes is not that women are credulous. It is that Gerry's language is a currency he can spend twice without devaluation, and that the sisters' isolation — no visitors, no other men, one wireless that works when it chooses — is what allows a counterfeit to pass. The play locates the flaw in the economy of the cottage rather than in the character of the women inside it, and that distinction is where its feminism is strongest.

Le geste. Même preuve, même observation, une seule chose change : la conclusion porte sur la technique de la pièce et la situation des personnages, plus sur les croyances de l'écrivain. On nomme aussi le procédé — structural dramatic irony — ce qui te fait gagner du crédit sur le critère « appreciation of the writer's method ». Et la phrase « it implicates us » est un mouvement de haut de barème : l'examinateur a explicitement crédité une copie du pack pour sa sensitivity to the audience response.

Version visant KP17–20
A KP17–20 version

To call Dancing at Lughnasa a feminist play is accurate about its diagnosis and misleading about its politics. No play of its decade is more exact about the machinery that closes around five unmarried women in 1936 Donegal: the hand-knitting that vanishes when a factory opens in the town, the schoolteacher's post held at a priest's discretion, the dance they are old enough to want and not permitted to attend.

But diagnosis is not advocacy. The sisters are never shown choosing constraint out of some weakness in them; they are shown with a range of available actions so narrow that the only exit Agnes and Rose find is to disappear into London and die there, and the play delivers that ending in a monologue, off stage, in the past tense, so that no one can intervene. If the play argues anything, it is that "why did they not leave?" is the wrong question. That is a colder feminism than one which supplies an answer, and a more demanding one.

Le geste. Une conclusion qui reformule au lieu de répéter. Elle rappelle le degré annoncé en introduction — accurate about its diagnosis, misleading about its politics — puis apporte une preuve neuve : le sort d'Agnes et Rose, raconté hors scène et au passé, de sorte que le public ne peut rien empêcher. C'est encore une preuve scénique, donc une réponse au mot play. Et la dernière phrase ouvre au lieu de fermer, ce qui est précisément ce que le barème appelle a mature, imaginative response.

The move. A conclusion that reframes instead of repeating. It restates the degree announced in the introduction — accurate about its diagnosis, misleading about its politics — then brings fresh evidence: the fate of Agnes and Rose, narrated off stage and in the past tense, so that the audience can prevent nothing. That is theatrical evidence again, and so an answer to the word play. And the last sentence opens rather than closes, which is exactly what the mark scheme calls a mature, imaginative response.
